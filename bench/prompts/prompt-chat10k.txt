Maya: A list of things I was going to do that summer. Learn to swim properly. Read four books. Visit my cousin. Fix the window in the spare room. I did one of them, which was reading the books, and I did that in the winter instead.

Tom: Lists are mostly wishes with numbers next to them.

Maya: That is a good line.

Tom: I stole it from a book.

Maya: Which book?

Tom: I do not remember. That is the problem with good lines. They stay and the book leaves.

Maya: I am writing it on a box. "Lists are wishes with numbers next to them." Then I will unpack the box in a year and find it and wonder what I meant.

Tom: That is how wisdom travels.

Maya: How is the sourdough experiment going? You were very proud of the starter.

Tom: The starter is thriving. It has a name now. The children next door call it Gerald. I do not know why. I did not name it. They came over one afternoon, looked at the jar, and decided it was Gerald, and the name stuck.

Maya: Gerald the starter. Does it behave?

Tom: It behaves like a small demanding pet. I feed it on a schedule. If I forget, it develops a layer of liquid on top and looks at me with disappointment. The bread is good, though. The last loaf had a crust that made a sound when I cut it, that crackling sound, and I stood in the kitchen listening to it like a fool.

Maya: That sound is the whole point of baking bread.

Tom: It is. You spend a day waiting and then you get four seconds of crackling. It is a terrible return on investment and completely worth it.

Maya: Did you try the rye flour?

Tom: I did, and it was heavy. Delicious, but heavy. The slices were so dense that I ate one and felt like I had eaten a meal. I have been mixing it half and half with the white flour and that seems to work.

Maya: My grandmother used to make a rye bread with caraway seeds. It was the color of a wet roof and it tasted like the inside of a church, in a good way.

Tom: The inside of a church in a good way is a very specific flavor.

Maya: You know what I mean. Dark and quiet and a little sweet. It made the whole house smell like winter.

Tom: I will try caraway next time. If it fails, I will blame you.

Maya: That is fair. I have been blamed for worse.

Tom: Have you thought about what you will do with the first weekend in the new place? After the boxes are gone and before real life starts again.

Maya: I have. I am going to do nothing. I am going to make coffee and sit in the courtyard and listen to the street and not unpack anything for one whole day. I have been moving toward this apartment for six months and I want to arrive properly.

Tom: That is a good plan. Most people skip the arriving part. They unpack and then immediately start worrying about the next thing.

Maya: I have done that every other time I moved. This time I am going to sit still. I might even be bored. I am looking forward to being bored.

Tom: Boredom is underrated. It is the only state in which I ever notice what I actually want.

Maya: That is a very good observation for a man who owns a starter named Gerald.

Tom: Gerald and I have deep conversations. He is a good listener. He never interrupts.

Maya: I went to the Saturday market this morning and came home with far too much cheese.

Tom: How much cheese is too much cheese?

Maya: Three kinds. A soft one that smells like a barn in the best way, a hard one with a rind the color of old paper, and something blue that the man insisted I try before buying. I tried it and then I bought it and then I walked around the rest of the market feeling slightly guilty.

Tom: That is not too much cheese. That is a reasonable amount of cheese for one person who likes cheese.

Maya: I also bought a bag of tiny tomatoes, a bunch of parsley, and a jar of honey from a woman who told me her bees live on a hill behind the church and that this year the flowers came late, so the honey is darker than usual.

Tom: Darker honey is usually better. It has more of everything.

Maya: She said the same thing. She said the late flowers make it taste like the end of summer, and I bought two jars because I did not want to run out of the end of summer.

Tom: You are turning into a person who buys two jars of honey at a market. I approve.

Maya: It is the new apartment. It has made me domestic. I have been looking at curtains online.

Tom: Curtains online is the first sign. Next you will have opinions about tea towels.

Maya: I already have opinions about tea towels. The thin ones that do not absorb anything are an insult. A tea towel should be thick enough to dry a plate in one pass.

Tom: This is a strong and correct opinion. My grandmother had tea towels that could dry an entire table of dishes and still feel dry themselves. I have never found any like them.

Maya: They probably came from a shop that closed in 1974. Everything good came from a shop that closed.

Tom: That is the truth. The best bakery in my hometown closed when I was twelve and I have spent twenty years comparing every loaf to a bread that no longer exists.

Maya: And nothing compares.

Tom: Nothing compares, and also I might be remembering it wrong. That is the other problem. The bread might have been ordinary. I was twelve and hungry and it was the first bread I ever bought with my own money.

Maya: The first anything is always the best. First bread, first bike, first terrible cup of coffee that you drank to feel grown up.

Tom: Mine was instant coffee with three spoons of sugar. It tasted like a burnt biscuit. I drank the whole cup and then could not sleep until two in the morning.

Maya: I did the same thing with tea. I made it far too strong and drank it while reading a book I did not understand, and I felt extremely sophisticated and also slightly ill.

Tom: That is the entire experience of being fifteen.

Maya: Speaking of being fifteen, my cousin sent me a photograph of the two of us at the lake, the summer we decided to build a raft out of barrels and pallets. We were so confident. The raft sank in about four minutes and we stood in the water arguing about whose fault it was.

Tom: Whose fault was it?

Maya: Mine. I was in charge of the rope. But I have denied it for twenty years and I am not going to stop now.

Tom: The rope is always the weak point. Anyone who has ever tied a mattress to a car knows this.

Maya: You tied a mattress to a car?

Tom: I tied a mattress to the roof of a borrowed car and drove it across town at twenty kilometers an hour with my arm out the window holding one corner. It was the most frightened I have ever been in a vehicle, including the time the brakes failed on a hill.

Maya: The brakes failed on a hill and the mattress was worse?

Tom: The brakes failed slowly, so there was time to think. The mattress was a live event. Every time a truck passed, the whole thing lifted and I could feel the car leaning.

Maya: What happened to the mattress?

Tom: It arrived. It was slightly deformed and had a mark on one corner from the roof rack, and I slept on it for three years. It was a good mattress. I still think about it sometimes.

Maya: You think about a mattress.

Tom: I think about the fact that I moved it three times and each time it was the hardest object in the apartment. Mattresses are the true test of friendship.

Maya: Then I am glad I only have a desk made from a door.

Tom: One more thing. What time should I come on Saturday?

Maya: Ten. The movers said they would be done by noon if the elevator cooperates, and it usually does. If it does not, we will be carrying boxes up three flights and you will regret offering.

Tom: I never regret offering. I only regret the piano.

Maya: There is no piano. There is a very heavy desk that my father made from a door, and I am considering leaving it behind as a gift to the next tenant.

Tom: A desk made from a door has stories in it. You cannot leave it.

Maya: I know. That is why it is coming with me, even though it weighs as much as a small car and the door it was made from was apparently the heaviest door in the county.

Tom: I will bring the hand truck and my good gloves. And the cake for the van man.

Maya: Bring the cake separately. I do not want it to smell like the inside of a van.

Tom: That is a reasonable request. I will also bring coffee for the morning, in the large flask, the one that keeps things hot until lunch.

Maya: You are a good friend.

Tom: I am a friend with a flask and access to a van. The rest is luck.

Maya: Ten o'clock. I will have the kettle on and the boxes labeled, or at least some of them labeled. The rest will be labeled "misc" and we will deal with them in five years.

Tom: I will bring a marker and label things properly while you are not looking. You will find a box that says "cables, definitely cables, do not open until 2031" and you will know it was me.

Maya: I will frame the label.

Tom: Now go to bed. You have nineteen boxes and a kitchen to pack.

Maya: Twenty. I found another one under the bed.

Tom: Of course you did.
